# PRIVÉE — staff-level audit

**Auditor's note on scope, read this first.** The audit brief describes a source tree
(`apps/privee/`, `build/rebuild.py`, `build/gate.py`, `build/hook_shipgate.py`,
`build/tests/`, `js/data.js`, `CLAUDE.md`). **None of it is in this repository.**
`mish5000/maison-x9k2m7q4` contains only the deployed GitHub Pages artifact:
`index.html` (1,544,086 bytes), `sw.js`, `dishes.json`, `lineups.json`,
`manifest.json`, `version.json`, `icon-*.png`, and `assets/` (2,004 files).
`list_repos` returns three repositories for this account and none is the PRIVÉE
source. So sections 3 (build/deploy gate) and 4 (test coverage) are **ungradable —
not "fine", ungradable.** I did not see `gate.py`, so I make no claim about it.

Everything below was verified by reading or executing the shipped artifact. The
1.14 MB app script was extracted, loaded into Node, and cross-checked
programmatically; two findings were reproduced in headless Chromium against a
local server. Where I could not verify, I say so.

One correction to the brief's premise: the deployed build does **not** inline
images. It inlines 7 WOFF2 fonts and 2 GIFs; all 1,795 referenced photographs are
external files under `assets/`. That is a better decision than the brief claims,
and it is why the SW's cache-on-view strategy is possible at all.

## The five most serious findings, ranked by blast radius

### 1. Positional keys are the join for 16 consumers, not 10 — and 3 of them live on the user's device where no build can ever migrate them

The brief calls this "the known structural weakness" and counts ten sidecars. The
real count is higher, and the extras are the dangerous ones.

**Verified inventory of positionally-keyed consumers (`"city|category|index"`):**

- **10 baked sidecars** — `VENUE_IMG` (828 keys), `VENUE_LL` (873), `VENUE_TEL`
  (662), `VENUE_WA` (20), `VENUE_GALLERY` (280), `VENUE_HOURS` (537), `VENUE_BP`
  (98), `VENUE_BOOK` (97), `VENUE_TAGS` (284), `VENUE_RATING` (688).
- **2 out-of-band JSON files** — `LINEUPS.venues` (18 baked / 13 in the live
  `lineups.json`) and `DISHES.venues` (213). These are **regenerated by bots on
  their own schedule and fetched at runtime, where they replace the baked seed
  wholesale.** `lineups.json` is stamped `2026-08-02T03:12:15Z`; the build is
  stamped `04:42 CEST`. They are produced by different processes at different
  times and nothing checks that they agree.
- **3 `localStorage` stores** — `privee_saved` (index.html:1864), `privee_plans`
  (1868), `privee_plan_times` (1880, composite key `iso|city|cat|idx`).

Total baked positional entries: **4,598** across **175 clusters**, **876 venues**,
**22 cities**.

**Blast radius, measured.** Inserting or deleting one venue re-points every
sidecar entry at a higher index in that cluster:

| Cluster | Venues | Sidecar entries | Entries re-pointed by an insert at index 0 |
|---|---|---|---|
| `london\|restaurants` | 27 | 211 | **211** |
| `marbella\|restaurants` | 23 | 158 | 158 |
| `losangeles\|restaurants` | 23 | 158 | 158 |
| `dubai\|restaurants` | 19 | 132 | 132 |
| `paris\|restaurants` | 17 | 117 | 117 |

Averaged over a uniformly random insert position, **one venue insert silently
re-points a mean of 13.0 sidecar entries**; a worst-case insert re-points 211.

**Why the localStorage three are the worst.** The baked sidecars can at least be
re-keyed atomically by the build — and the evidence below shows the build usually
does. `localStorage` cannot. `viewSaved()` (index.html:3560) resolves a persisted
key with:

```js
const [cityId, catId, idx] = k.split('|');
const city = CITY_BY_ID[cityId];
const v = city?.venues?.[catId]?.[+idx];
return v ? { city, catId, v, idx: +idx } : null;
}).filter(Boolean);
```

No name check, no checksum. If the venue at that index changed, the member's saved
list silently shows **a different restaurant**. If the index fell off the end,
`.filter(Boolean)` **silently deletes it from their list with no message.** The
same pattern governs dated plans (3510) and the shared-week import (3487).

**Safe vs. one edit away.** Nothing in the shipped app mutates these arrays — they
are literals. The mutation surface is entirely in the build tooling I cannot see.
What I *can* say from the artifact: `VENUE_IMG` was re-keyed correctly (evidence
in finding 2), so a re-keying mechanism exists and mostly works; four sidecars
were missed once; and no mechanism exists, anywhere, for the three device-side
stores.

### 2. A venue is live in production right now showing another venue's photographs, phone number and rating

**This is not hypothetical. It is deployed.** `losangeles|beach` is the one
cluster of 175 where sidecar keys exceed the array length:

```
losangeles|beach  arrayLen=2
  VENUE_IMG   max key 1     VENUE_LL   max key 1     VENUE_HOURS max key 1
  VENUE_TEL   max key 2  ←  VENUE_GALLERY max key 2  ←
  VENUE_BP    max key 2  ←  VENUE_RATING  max key 2  ←
```

The array holds two venues: `Little Beach House Malibu`, `Malibu Beach Inn`. Four
sidecars still carry an index-2 entry the array can no longer reach.

**The proof that this is misalignment and not merely stranded data** needs no
external knowledge. The build names assets `v_<city>-<cat>-<N>.jpg` and
`g_<city>-<cat>-<N>-<k>.jpg` from the *same* N. Rendered live in Chromium at
`#/cat/losangeles/beachpools`, the card for `losangeles|beach|0`
(*Little Beach House Malibu*) shows:

```
hero    : assets/v_losangeles-beach-1.jpg    ← original index 1
gallery : assets/g_losangeles-beach-0-0.jpg  ← original index 0
          assets/g_losangeles-beach-0-1.jpg
phone   : tel:+1310-317-9140                 ← VENUE_TEL key 0
rating  : ★ 4.3 · 3,642                      ← VENUE_RATING key 0
```

**One card cannot have a hero from slot 1 and a gallery from slot 0 and be
correct.** One of them belongs to another property. Opening the two files
confirms they depict different places — the hero is an aerial of a large pool
deck with yellow cabanas; the gallery is an oceanfront dining terrace.

**Reconstruction:** the cluster originally held three venues. Index 0 was deleted.
`VENUE_IMG`, `VENUE_LL` and `VENUE_HOURS` were re-keyed down by one;
`VENUE_TEL`, `VENUE_GALLERY`, `VENUE_BP` and `VENUE_RATING` were not. So today
*Little Beach House Malibu* displays the deleted venue's phone, gallery, badge and
rating; *Malibu Beach Inn* displays *Little Beach House*'s; and the real data for
*Malibu Beach Inn* sits orphaned at key 2, never shown. The deleted venue's hero,
`assets/v_losangeles-beach-0.jpg`, is still on disk, unreferenced.

**Reproduction:** serve the repo, open `#/cat/losangeles/beachpools`, tap the
first card. The phone number dialled is not the venue's.

**Scale of the historical pattern.** Comparing the index encoded in every
self-describing filename against its key finds **18 `VENUE_IMG` entries** whose
file index differs from their key — in `london|restaurants` (16→17, 17→18, 18→19,
19→20), `losangeles|restaurants` (10→11 … 13→14), `dubai|resorts`, `dubai|lounges`,
`marbella|beach`, `mykonos|restaurants`, `sttropez|nightclubs`, and
`losangeles|lounges` (delta **2**, i.e. two deletions). Every one is a past
renumber. In 17 of 18 the whole pipeline kept up. In one it did not, and that one
is still serving wrong data.

**Credit where due:** an independent check of all 39 hostname-derived image
filenames (`v_https_www_claridges_co_uk.jpg` etc.) against each venue's own `w`
field found **39/39 correct**, and semantic spot-checks confirm alignment —
`london|restaurants|0` is Restaurant Gordon Ramsay and its dishes list the
lobster/langoustine/salmon ravioli; `london|restaurants|1` is Core by Clare Smyth
with "Potato and roe"; `ibiza|nightclubs|0` is Hï Ibiza with a Black
Coffee/CamelPhat bill. **The dataset is overwhelmingly correct.** That is exactly
what makes the one wrong card dangerous: nothing distinguishes it.

### 3. Every "OPEN · UNTIL" badge in 18 of 20 cities becomes wrong by one hour on 25 October 2026

`VENUE_HOURS` stores the venue's timezone as a **fixed integer offset**, and
`openState()` (index.html:2164) consumes it as one:

```js
const local = new Date(Date.now() + (h.o + new Date().getTimezoneOffset()) * 60000);
```

The **viewer's** offset is read live and follows DST correctly. The **venue's**
offset is frozen at build time. All 537 entries carry a numeric offset, and every
one is the summer value:

```
london 60 (BST; winter 0)          paris/marbella/madrid/milan/monaco/ibiza/
                                   capri/courchevel/portocervo/sttropez 120 (CEST; winter 60)
mykonos/bodrum 180 (EEST; 120)     newyork/miami/hamptons -240 (EDT; -300)
losangeles -420 (PDT; -480)        aspen -360 (MDT; -420)
dubai 240 ✓ no DST                 stbarths -240 ✓ no DST
```

**On 25 October 2026 (EU) and 1 November 2026 (US) every one of those cities goes
stale by exactly 60 minutes**, and nothing in the app can notice. The failure is
maximally dishonest: for the hour after closing the app says **"OPEN · UNTIL
23:00"**, and for the first hour it is genuinely open it says **closed**. Two
Dubai/St Barths cities are correct year-round only because those zones have no DST.

The same frozen offset drives `venueTonight()` (index.html:1977), which decides
that a club night belongs to the day it opens by testing `getUTCHours() < 6`. In
winter that boundary lands an hour off, so between 05:00 and 06:00 local the app
serves **the wrong night's DJ line-up** — confidently, with a highlighted headliner.

**Reproduction:** set the container clock to 2026-11-01, load any London venue with
hours, compare the badge to the venue's real hours. I did not run this (I did not
want to move the system clock mid-audit) — the mechanism is read from source and
is unambiguous, but I mark the runtime reproduction **unverified**.

### 4. Verified XSS: a shared-week link executes arbitrary script in the app origin

`esc()` is the app's only escaper (index.html:2243):

```js
const esc = (s) => s.replace(/"/g, '&quot;');
```

It escapes double quotes and nothing else — not `<`, not `>`, not `&`. That is an
attribute-value quoter, not an HTML escaper. For author-controlled venue copy that
is survivable. But `viewWeek(b64)` (index.html:3501) base64-decodes a payload
**from the URL hash** and interpolates attacker-controlled fields straight into
`$app.innerHTML`:

```js
<h1 class="display serif rise">${esc(p.n || 'A member')}’s week</h1>
```

`p.n` is the sharer's display name. `tr.hl`, `tr.sub`, `tr.tm` (trip rows) and
`tm` (plan times) are equally attacker-controlled. The only guard is
`typeof p.d === 'object'`, trivially satisfied.

**Reproduction — executed, not reasoned about.** Payload
`{"n":"<img src=x onerror=alert(document.domain)>","d":{}}`, base64url-encoded:

```
#/week/eyJuIjoiPGltZyBzcmM9eCBvbmVycm9yPWFsZXJ0KGRvY3VtZW50LmRvbWFpbik-IiwiZCI6e319
```

Loaded in headless Chromium against `http://127.0.0.1:8899/index.html`:

```
ALERT FIRED: "127.0.0.1"
h1 innerHTML: <img src="x" onerror="alert(document.domain)">’s week
injected <img> elements in h1: 1
```

**Honest severity: moderate, not critical.** The app is `noindex,nofollow`, has no
backend, no API keys and no credentials — consistent with the constitution as the
brief describes it. The realistic impact is reading and exfiltrating
`privee_saved` / `privee_plans` (the member's movements — for this app's threat
model, that is the sensitive asset) and defacing the view. It requires the member
to open a crafted link — but the share-a-week feature exists precisely to make
members open such links, and `tinyify()` (3121) shortens them, hiding the payload.
**The fix is one line:** make `esc` escape `&<>"'`.

### 5. The service worker adds a permanent cache entry on every single app launch

`sw.js` handles `dishes.json` through the generic branch and `lineups.json` /
`version.json` through the sidecar branch. Both branches call `cache.put(req, …)`
keyed on the **full request URL** — and the app fetches all three with a
cache-busting query:

```js
fetch('lineups.json?ts=' + Date.now(), { cache: 'no-store' })   // 1930
fetch('dishes.json?ts='  + Date.now(), { cache: 'no-store' })   // 1947
fetch('version.json?ts=' + Date.now(), { cache: 'no-store' })   // 5242
```

`caches.match()` defaults to `ignoreSearch: false`, so every launch misses, fetches,
and **stores a new entry under a URL that will never be requested again.** Nothing
evicts them. The `activate` handler deletes *other* cache names, not entries within
`privee-shell-v2`.

`dishes.json` is **137,951 bytes**. That is ~138 KB of dead cache per launch, plus
19 KB for `lineups.json`, plus a `version.json` entry per foreground resume
(throttled to one a minute, 5305). Roughly **100 launches ≈ 15 MB of garbage**
against the ~50 MB iOS quota that `sw.js`'s own header comment identifies as the
constraint it is designed around. The consequence is the one the file explicitly
tries to prevent: quota pressure that stops the shell from caching, silently
disabling offline mode.

It is self-limiting only by accident — `swFlush()` wipes the whole cache on a
version bump (5253), which also throws away every offline photo the member had
accumulated.

**Reproduction:** load the app N times, inspect
`caches.open('privee-shell-v2').keys()`; entry count grows by ≥3 per launch. I
verified the code path and the arithmetic by reading `sw.js` and the three fetch
sites; I did **not** run the N-launch experiment. Marked **partially verified**.

## Other verified issues, below the top five

- **`LINEUPS` is replaced wholesale, not merged** (1932: `LINE = j`). The live
  `lineups.json` covers 13 venues; the baked seed covers 18. On every successful
  fetch, **DC-10 · Circoloco, Tibu Banús, Pangea, VOID and Club Space silently
  lose their line-ups.** Defensible if the bot is authoritative, but it is a
  silent narrowing with no log.
- **`.herosearch` uses unprefixed `backdrop-filter: blur(10px)`** with no
  `-webkit-` sibling. Silently no-ops on iOS Safari < 16 — cosmetic only.
- **`+34 682 11 22 33`** is shared by six unrelated Marbella venues (La Plaza, Sea
  Grill, LPM, Chambao, El Chiringuito, COYA Club). A Spanish *mobile* prefix with
  a sequential 11-22-33 body looks like a placeholder. **Unverified** — I did not
  call it. The other 38 shared phone numbers and all 48 shared coordinate pairs
  are legitimate (a hotel and its pool, spa and bar share a switchboard).
- **203 asset files are unreferenced.** The build does not garbage-collect. Zero
  referenced assets are missing, so no broken images.
- **`tryAutoUpdate()` compares build stamps with `!==`, not ordering** (5245). A
  stale or rolled-back `version.json` triggers one reload per session, forever.

## Section 5 — Readability and maintainability: B−

**Genuinely good, and unusual.** The comments explain *why*, not *what*, and they
carry provenance and dates: *"IO, NEVER a scroll listener: the old rAF handler ran
`getBoundingClientRect`"* (4231); *"it re-samples EVERY momentum-scroll frame
(WebKit audit F1, 23 Jul)"*; *"the fire mark must not promise what it cannot
deliver"*; *"'Nightclub' was the one false entry: Delilah and San Vicente Bungalows
are late rooms, not nightclubs."* A cold engineer learns the reasoning behind the
constraints, which is rarer and more valuable than clean abstractions.
`PART_TO_DISPLAY` (built from `CATEGORIES` so old deep links keep resolving after a
category merge) is a small piece of real craft.

**What costs it the grade.** One 7,774-line script with no module boundaries.
Venue records use single-letter keys — `n, a, ad, b, w, p, t, wa, img` — with no
schema in the shipped artifact; a newcomer must infer that `b` is the blurb and
`ad` the street address by reading call sites. Every sidecar read is guarded with
`typeof X !== 'undefined' && X[key]`, ~30 times, defending against a condition
that cannot occur in a single-file build — noise that trains the reader to ignore
the guard that might one day matter.

## Section 6 — Performance: B (mechanisms only, no numbers)

**Right, and deliberately so.** Only **2** `getBoundingClientRect` calls in
application code (4739, 6946) — no read/write interleaving, no thrash loop. **8**
`IntersectionObserver`s replacing scroll handlers, with the reason recorded in a
comment. **21 `@keyframes` blocks, zero of which animate a layout- or
paint-triggering property** — all transform/opacity, all compositable. That is
better discipline than most production CSS. `content-visibility` used in 4 places.
Image caching is bounded by what the member actually browses rather than
precached. DOM stays lean: **250 nodes** on home, **869** on the largest feed
(`london|restaurants`, 27 cards), ~6–7 MB JS heap.

**The real per-frame costs.** **42 `backdrop-filter` rules**, six of which sit on
elements that also transition, animate or transform (`#sheet-backdrop`, `#gzoom`,
`.pulsebtn`, `.fchip`, the bottom nav, the map home pill). On WebKit a moving
element over a backdrop root re-samples the backdrop every frame; that is the
dominant compositing cost here and the thing to watch on real hardware. The
project clearly knows this — the `.vcard .save` comment documents removing exactly
this pattern after a prior audit — so the remaining six are presumably judged
worth it, but they are the hot spots. Secondary: **1.14 MB of JavaScript parsed on
every cold start**, comprising 876 venue literals, 4,598 sidecar entries and a full
inlined Leaflet. That is the single largest startup cost and it is paid before
first paint (the boot loader exists to cover it).

Per the brief, no fps figures: this container is software-rendered and any number
I produced would be fiction.

## The three highest-leverage refactors

### A. Give every venue a stable `id`; make positional keys a build-time artifact
**Cost: 2–4 days, mostly mechanical, needs the source tree.**
Add `id: 'la-little-beach-house'` to each of the 876 records. Re-key all 10
sidecars and both JSON feeds to it. The bots that write `lineups.json` and
`dishes.json` emit ids, so a build-time renumber can never desynchronise them
again. Ship a one-time `localStorage` migration mapping old positional keys to ids
on first launch of the new build (this is the only chance to save members' existing
saved lists and plans — after this the window closes, because a later renumber
makes the mapping unrecoverable). This kills findings 1 and 2 permanently and is
the only refactor here that removes a whole *class* of failure rather than an
instance. The mechanical part is easy; the migration is the part that needs care.

### B. Add an integrity assertion to the ship gate
**Cost: half a day. Highest value per hour on this list.**
A script that loads `data.js` **by executing it, never by regex** — the brief flags
regex parsing as the thing that has already caused two incidents, and I agree it is
the right thing to distrust, though I could not inspect the build scripts to
confirm where it happens — and asserts:
1. every sidecar key resolves to an in-range index (**catches today's
   `losangeles|beach` bug in under a second**);
2. every self-describing asset filename's index equals its key's index
   (**catches all 18 historical shift sites**);
3. every hostname-derived image matches its venue's `w` field (39 checks, all
   currently passing — this is a free regression net);
4. `lineups.json` / `dishes.json` keys all resolve.
Wire it into the gate and it becomes impossible to ship finding 2 again. Do this
one first — it is cheap, it is a genuine safety net, and it protects you while
refactor A is in flight.

### C. Store IANA zone names instead of frozen UTC offsets
**Cost: 1 day.**
Replace `"o": 120` with `"tz": "Europe/Madrid"` across 537 `VENUE_HOURS` entries
(derivable from the city, so it is a 22-line lookup table, not 537 hand edits) and
compute venue-local time with `Intl.DateTimeFormat(…, {timeZone})`. Fixes finding 3
for every city, permanently, including the club-night rollover. There is a genuine
deadline: this must land before **25 October 2026** or the app spends the winter
lying about opening hours.

*(The `esc()` fix in finding 4 is not on this list because it is a one-line change,
not a refactor. Do it today.)*

## Would I let this ship to paying users as it stands?

Not as it stands — but the gap is days of work, not a rewrite, and that is worth
saying plainly. This is a thoughtfully built application by someone who has
clearly been burned before and wrote down what they learned: the WebKit
compositing discipline is real and documented with dates, the service worker's
"never stop the app from loading" design is correct and rare, the fail-safe data
rules ("no dishes, no button", "real photo or nothing", "a plausible-but-wrong
photo is worse than no photo") are exactly the right instincts, and 4,594 of 4,598
positional keys are correct. The problem is that those instincts are written in
comments rather than enforced in code, and where the enforcement gap opened, the
app did the one thing its own constitution forbids: it served a plausible, wrong
answer with total confidence. For a concierge product whose entire value is that
the member trusts it enough to *stop checking*, a card that dials the wrong number
and shows another hotel's photographs is not a cosmetic bug — it is the product
failing at the only thing it does. Three blockers: fix the `losangeles|beach`
misalignment, make `esc()` a real escaper, and land the timezone fix before late
October. Add refactor B's integrity check to the gate so the first one cannot
recur. That is roughly a week. After that I would ship it without hesitation, and
I would be reasonably confident in it — which is more than I can say for most
things I audit at this size.

**And I would not sign off on sections 3 and 4 at all until someone shows me
`build/`.** The brief asks whether the ship gate is real or theatre. I cannot
answer that. On the evidence available, a gate that was doing its job would have
caught `losangeles|beach` — four sidecar keys pointing past the end of a
two-element array is a check any integrity assertion would make, and it reached
production anyway. That is not proof the gate is theatre; it is proof that
whatever the gate checks, it does not check this.
